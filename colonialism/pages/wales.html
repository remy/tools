Wales

Devolved Nation (UK)

Wales was conquered by England in the 13th century under Edward I. The Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) brought Wales under English law. Welsh language and culture were systematically suppressed for centuries. Today Wales has a devolved Senedd with limited powers, but Westminster retains sovereignty.

Key events

1282
Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, last native Prince of Wales, killed by English forces.
1284
Statute of Rhuddlan — Wales formally annexed to the English Crown.
1536
Laws in Wales Acts (1535–1542) merge Wales with England. English is made the sole language of law and administration, and monoglot Welsh speakers are barred from public office.
1847
The 'Blue Books' report condemns Welsh language and culture as backward — a turning point in cultural suppression known as Brad y Llyfrau Gleision (Treachery of the Blue Books).
1993
Welsh Language Act gives Welsh equal status with English in public life.
1999
Devolution — the Senedd Cymru (Welsh Parliament) is established.

Atrocities & injustices

⚠ What the history books often omit

Cultural suppression was the primary tool: the Welsh Not was a wooden block hung around the neck of any child caught speaking Welsh at school. The child would pass it to the next Welsh speaker, and whoever wore it at the end of the day was beaten. This practice continued into the late 19th century. The flooding of the Tryweryn valley in 1965, drowning the Welsh village of Capel Celyn to create a reservoir for Liverpool — with no Welsh MP vote in favour — remains a symbol of Westminster's disregard.

Today

The present

Wales has a devolved parliament with powers over health, education and some taxation. The 2021 Census recorded 17.8% of people (538,300) able to speak Welsh — a figure that actually fell from 19.0% in 2011, despite the Welsh Government's target of a million speakers by 2050. (Self-reported survey estimates run higher, around 29%, but the census is the official measure.) There is a growing independence movement; polling in 2024–25 (Redfield & Wilton, YouGov) put support at roughly 37–41% once undecided voters are excluded, up sharply from a decade ago. Wales voted narrowly for Brexit in 2016, which many Welsh nationalists found surprising given the EU's support for minority languages.
