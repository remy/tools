Iraq

Independent Republic

Britain created Iraq from three Ottoman provinces after WWI, drawing borders that cut across ethnic and religious communities. The country was given a British-imposed monarchy and remained under British influence until the 1958 revolution. The borders Britain drew are central to understanding Iraq's modern sectarian conflicts.

Key events

1920
British Mandate for Mesopotamia. Major revolt — the 1920 Revolution — suppressed including by RAF bombing of villages.
1921
Britain installs Faisal I as King. Churchill chairs the Cairo Conference that draws the borders.
1932
Iraq gains nominal independence but Britain retains military bases and influence.
1941
Britain re-occupies Iraq during WWII after a pro-Axis coup.
1958
Revolution. The British-installed monarchy is overthrown.

Atrocities & injustices

⚠ What the history books often omit

As War Secretary, Winston Churchill explicitly endorsed using poison gas against rebellious tribes in Iraq — 'I am strongly in favour of using poisoned gas against uncivilised tribes... [to] spread a lively terror.' Whether gas was actually deployed against Kurds and Arabs is disputed: most historians (e.g. R.M. Douglas) conclude it was authorised but never used in practice, with the RAF instead using conventional bombing to police and terrorise Iraqi villages. Britain amalgamated Sunni, Shia, and Kurdish populations into a single state designed for British strategic and economic purposes, creating tensions that have never been resolved.

Today

The present

Iraq has experienced dictatorship under Saddam Hussein, the Iran-Iraq War, the Gulf War, the 2003 US/UK invasion (itself a colonial-style intervention), and the rise of ISIS — all traceable in various ways to the colonial state Britain created.
